Design thesis

Why we put a pipeline in the middle

Most systems that touch service requests do it one of three ways, and all three lose context at the seam. AI made a fourth option viable: draw a line, put the pipeline there, and every downstream system gets the request already analyzed instead of raw.

The three defaults

How most operations wire this today

There are three shapes an intake-to-fulfillment operation usually takes. Each one works. Each one costs something the others don't. Verdikt is a fourth shape, made possible by AI carrying context that used to be a human's job.

Default 1

Everything inside the CRM

Case management platform becomes the system-of-record for intake, decisioning, correspondence, and fulfillment orchestration. Great for reporting; every change is a CRM build. Rules are hard-coded, letters live in template tools, decisions are humans clicking buttons.

Costs: every new program or channel is a project. Rules and letters drift from the CRM code that reads them.

Default 2

One-off per channel

Email intake gets its own scripts. Fax has its own OCR pipeline. Portal writes directly to the case DB. EHR integrations go through a middleware. Each channel is normalized differently before landing in the same case store.

Costs: five extract-and-map codepaths instead of one. Bugs discovered in one channel don't automatically get fixed in the others.

Default 3

Siloed teams stitching systems

Intake team hands off to an eligibility team, who hands off to a fulfillment team, who hands off to a compliance team. Every hand-off is an email, a spreadsheet, or a shared inbox. The case is the same; the context isn't carried across.

Costs: humans re-read the same documents at every step. Turnaround measured in days, not seconds.
Why now

The bit that used to be the human's job

All three defaults exist because context, what does this case actually mean, is it decidable, which rules apply, what should happen next, used to be something only a human could produce reliably. So every architecture pushed context work to the humans and let the systems be dumb pipes.

Modern extraction, retrieval, and reasoning models change what a machine can carry across a seam. The intake documents can be read into structured fields with fidelity. The program rules can be retrieved and cited. The verdict can be produced with an explanation attached. Not perfectly, but reliably enough that the downstream systems can trust context has been established and act on it.

Once that's true, the shape of the operation can change. The pipeline in the middle stops being aspirational.

The line we drew

A logical boundary, not another silo

The line runs between "reading the case in context" and "writing the reply." AI carries the case across the first half; deterministic code carries the reply across the second half. Everything upstream of the line normalizes into one case; everything downstream receives it already analyzed.

The pipeline is the seam. AI stops before the reply is written; the deterministic render carries bytes-forever guarantees to every downstream integration.

What this unlocks

Five things that were structural blockers before

These aren't features; they're properties of the shape. Once the line is drawn, the rest follows.

Every request enters analyzed

The pipeline reads the case, cites the rules, and produces the verdict before anything downstream sees it. Downstream systems act on meaning, not raw attachments.

Before: CRM stored PDFs; a human opened each one to decide.

Context flows across the seam

The verdict, the rationale, the rule citations, and the byte-identical reply travel together as one payload. When a CRM ticket opens, it's already annotated. When Pharmacy OMS receives a dispense order, it knows why.

Before: humans re-explained the case at every hand-off.

New channels plug in without a rebuild

Fax, mail, portal, EHR all normalize into the same case shape before the pipeline runs. Adding a channel means writing one adapter, not rewriting the whole decision path.

Before: each channel had its own extract-and-map codepath.

New programs onboard as data

Program rules, extraction schemas, and reply templates are configuration, not code. A second program with a stricter cap and a different template is a folder, not a project.

Before: each new program was a CRM release cycle.

Triage is a pipeline stage, not a human job

The pipeline classifies each case (new intake · refill · appeal · missing-info · not-decidable) and routes on what's actually inside. Refills short-circuit to Pharmacy OMS; missing-info goes straight to CRM outreach; fresh intakes continue down the full Decide → Deliver path.

Before: a human opened every envelope just to know what the case was.
The core idea

The design, in three principles

Zoom out and the whole shape reduces to three choices: where AI belongs, how it's deployed safely, and where humans belong. Together, they let the operation pull the real power of AI while keeping the deterministic, auditable outcomes this industry demands.

  1. 01

    AI upstream, deterministic downstream

    The CRM and every downstream system stay AI-free. They receive verdicts, replies, and cited rules as data. All the AI work lives upstream of the line, moving cases toward a decision instead of summarizing decisions after the fact. The audited, regulated part of the stack never has to reason.

  2. 02

    AI in the right place, with fallbacks

    Not every step is an AI step. The pipeline picks the stages where AI genuinely adds signal (read, triage, verdict rationale) and leaves the rest deterministic. Every AI call has a fallback path so the pipeline degrades safely, not stops.

  3. 03

    Humans in / on / over the loop

    In-the-loop for edge cases the model isn't confident about. On-the-loop for silent oversight of routine cases. Over-the-loop for the reviews that actually need judgment: exceptions, appeals, trend calls. Humans stop doing the reading. They start doing the reviewing.
